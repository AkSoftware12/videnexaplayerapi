Why MKV and HEVC fail on some players

MKV is a container — it can hold video, several audio tracks and subtitles together. HEVC (H.265) is a modern, highly compressed video codec. Many stock players only support a narrow set of codecs, so they either refuse the file or play video without audio.

Good to know: The file extension (.mkv) isn't the problem — it's whether your player can decode what's inside it. That's why the same file plays on one app and fails on another.

What's actually inside an MKV file

An MKV file is a container, and its extension tells you very little. Inside it are separate streams: one video stream, one or more audio streams, and sometimes subtitle tracks. Each stream uses its own codec.

That's why two files that both end in .mkv can behave completely differently. One opens on the first tap and the other fails, because the codecs inside are different. The combination that plays almost everywhere is H.264 video with AAC audio. Trouble usually starts with HEVC (H.265) video, 10-bit colour, or a less common audio codec.

Why HEVC is harder to play

HEVC compresses video far more efficiently than the older H.264 codec, so files are often much smaller at similar quality. The trade-off is that decoding it needs more processing power. Newer phones have dedicated hardware for it, but some older and budget phones don't. On those, you may see a black screen with sound, or playback that stutters. A player that can also decode HEVC in software gives those phones a way to play the file. HEVC vs MKV: what's the difference?

They aren't two competing formats. MKV is a container, the "box" that holds the video, one or more audio tracks and subtitles. HEVC (H.265) is a codec, the method used to compress the video picture inside that box. An .mkv file can contain HEVC video, and HEVC video can also sit inside an .mp4 or .mov file. So when a file "won't play", the problem is usually the codec inside, not the .mkv extension.
